# UX Core pattern cards

Compact working cards for the 105 English KeepSimple UX Core patterns. Use them as hypotheses for design decisions, not as proof that a particular treatment will work. Source and full examples: https://keepsimple.io/uxcore

Format: **pattern** — meaning. **Use:** practical UI application. **Risk:** what to avoid.

## Attention, perception, and memory

- **Availability heuristics** — memorable or recent examples feel more likely. **Use:** explain unfamiliar value with concrete, relevant examples. **Risk:** hide neither real risks nor negative evidence.
- **Attentional bias** — attention is pulled toward emotionally salient signals. **Use:** reserve emphasis for the next important action. **Risk:** alarm users with irrelevant badges or urgency.
- **Illusory truth effect** — repeated claims feel more credible. **Use:** repeat stable terminology and key guidance consistently. **Risk:** repetition is not evidence.
- **Mere-exposure effect** — familiarity can increase preference. **Use:** keep navigation, labels, and patterns consistent. **Risk:** do not use repetition to normalize unwanted consent.
- **Context effect** — surrounding context changes interpretation. **Use:** place explanation, cost, and consequences next to the decision. **Risk:** never isolate important caveats.
- **Cue-dependent forgetting** — recall improves with useful cues. **Use:** show saved values, examples, and inline help. **Risk:** do not make users reconstruct prior context.
- **Mood-congruent memory bias** — current mood affects recalled information. **Use:** make stressful flows calm and recovery-oriented. **Risk:** avoid emotionally exploiting vulnerable moments.
- **Frequency illusion** — recently noticed things seem more common. **Use:** validate product trends with data, not anecdotes. **Risk:** do not overreact to a few visible reports.
- **Picture superiority effect** — images can be recalled better than words. **Use:** use purposeful diagrams or previews for complex concepts. **Risk:** decorative imagery must not replace critical text.
- **Von Restorff effect** — distinctive items attract recall. **Use:** highlight one primary action or exceptional state. **Risk:** competing highlights destroy hierarchy.
- **Self-reference effect** — personally relevant information is remembered better. **Use:** show the user's goal, data, or role in copy. **Risk:** personalization must be accurate and transparent.
- **Negativity bias** — negative information receives disproportionate weight. **Use:** make errors, risks, and recovery clear without dramatization. **Risk:** avoid fear-based conversion.
- **Anchoring effect** — the first number or frame influences later judgment. **Use:** establish honest scope, price context, or baseline. **Risk:** do not anchor users with arbitrary inflated claims.
- **Conservatism (belief revision)** — people update beliefs slowly. **Use:** support migrations with comparisons and gradual change. **Risk:** do not treat resistance as ignorance.
- **Contrast effect** — an option is judged against nearby alternatives. **Use:** compare meaningful tradeoffs side by side. **Risk:** do not create fake contrast with a deliberately bad decoy.
- **Distinction bias** — options feel different in comparison more than alone. **Use:** provide direct comparison for plans and features. **Risk:** preserve standalone explanations for each option.
- **Framing effect** — presentation changes decisions. **Use:** explain outcomes in user-relevant language and show both gains and costs. **Risk:** do not conceal the equivalent loss frame.
- **Money illusion** — nominal amounts distract from real value. **Use:** show total cost, frequency, and comparable units. **Risk:** avoid tiny-period pricing that hides totals.
- **Weber-Fechner Law** — perceived change depends on relative, not absolute, difference. **Use:** make meaningful changes visually and verbally clear. **Risk:** do not exaggerate tiny differences.
- **Serial-position effect** — first and last items are recalled best. **Use:** put important navigation and summary items at the beginning or end. **Risk:** long lists still need grouping.
- **Primacy effect** — early information has extra influence. **Use:** make the first explanation accurate and useful. **Risk:** do not front-load marketing before essential context.
- **List-length effect** — list size affects perceived difficulty and recall. **Use:** group, filter, and summarize long lists. **Risk:** do not hide important items behind arbitrary pagination.
- **Serial recall** — ordered information is remembered sequentially. **Use:** use numbered steps for procedures. **Risk:** do not force a sequence when users need exploration.
- **Peak-end rule** — experiences are judged by peak moments and the ending. **Use:** make completion, success, and handoff helpful. **Risk:** one polished ending cannot excuse a broken core flow.
- **Fading affect bias** — emotional intensity fades, often faster for negative events. **Use:** provide durable records and follow-up for important incidents. **Risk:** do not assume users will forget harm.
- **Less-is-better effect** — a smaller offer can be preferred when presented clearly. **Use:** make the simplest suitable option easy to choose. **Risk:** do not remove necessary capability to create an artificial premium.
- **Processing difficulty effect** — difficult-to-process information can feel less familiar or credible. **Use:** improve readability, structure, and terminology. **Risk:** complexity is not a trust signal.
- **The Magical Number 7±2** — working memory is limited. **Use:** chunk controls and choices into manageable groups. **Risk:** treat the number as a heuristic, not a hard UI limit.
- **Illusion of transparency** — people overestimate how clearly their intent is understood. **Use:** confirm important choices and expose state. **Risk:** never assume an icon communicates intent.
- **Curse of knowledge** — experts forget what beginners do not know. **Use:** test copy with first-time users and explain domain terms. **Risk:** avoid jargon-heavy empty states and errors.
- **Spotlight effect** — people overestimate how much others notice them. **Use:** reduce unnecessary social exposure and make privacy clear. **Risk:** do not use embarrassment to drive action.
- **Illusion of asymmetric insight** — people overestimate their understanding of others. **Use:** validate personas with research and feedback. **Risk:** do not design from stereotypes.

## Judgment, evidence, and decision-making

- **Omission bias** — inaction can feel safer than action. **Use:** explain the cost of waiting and make safe action reversible. **Risk:** do not manufacture guilt.
- **Base rate fallacy** — vivid specifics outweigh general probability. **Use:** show representative rates and sample size. **Risk:** do not lead with exceptional testimonials.
- **Confirmation bias** — people seek evidence supporting existing beliefs. **Use:** expose counter-evidence and alternative filters. **Risk:** do not personalize only to reinforce assumptions.
- **Congruence bias** — tests often confirm an existing hypothesis rather than challenge it. **Use:** design research and empty states that surface disconfirming evidence. **Risk:** do not interpret one successful path as validation.
- **Illusory correlation** — unrelated events are perceived as linked. **Use:** distinguish correlation from causation in analytics. **Risk:** avoid dashboards that imply unsupported relationships.
- **Hot hand fallacy** — recent success is seen as evidence of continuing success. **Use:** show stable performance windows and uncertainty. **Risk:** do not encourage risky escalation.
- **Gambler's fallacy** — independent events are expected to compensate for prior outcomes. **Use:** clarify randomness and probabilities. **Risk:** avoid streak-based pressure.
- **Recency illusion** — recent changes seem more important than they are. **Use:** provide historical context and comparison periods. **Risk:** do not let the latest metric dominate automatically.
- **Illusion of validity** — confidence in a judgment exceeds the quality of evidence. **Use:** expose confidence, limitations, and data quality. **Risk:** avoid authoritative dashboards without methodology.
- **Anecdotal evidence** — a vivid story is mistaken for representative evidence. **Use:** pair testimonials with aggregate data and context. **Risk:** one story must not justify a broad claim.
- **Neglect of probability** — possibility is confused with likelihood. **Use:** communicate probability and severity separately. **Risk:** do not use rare worst cases as routine pressure.
- **Insensitivity to sample size** — small samples are treated as stable. **Use:** show sample size and uncertainty. **Risk:** avoid declaring winners too early.
- **Clustering illusion** — random patterns look meaningful. **Use:** offer trend lines and baseline comparisons. **Risk:** do not highlight random streaks as insight.
- **Bias blind spot** — people notice bias in others more than themselves. **Use:** include neutral review checkpoints and alternative interpretations. **Risk:** do not present the product as bias-free.
- **Continued influence effect** — rejected misinformation can keep affecting decisions. **Use:** replace false information with a clear corrected model. **Risk:** repeating the false claim can reinforce it.
- **Subjective validation** — vague statements feel personally accurate. **Use:** make recommendations specific, testable, and user-editable. **Risk:** avoid Barnum-style personalization.
- **Ostrich effect** — people avoid information that may be unpleasant. **Use:** make risk status visible and provide calm next steps. **Risk:** do not hide bad news behind optimistic UI.
- **Observer-expectancy effect** — expectations influence observation and interpretation. **Use:** blind or standardize evaluations where possible. **Risk:** avoid leading research questions.
- **Selective perception** — people notice information matching their focus. **Use:** provide summaries and configurable views. **Risk:** do not let a default view hide important counter-signals.
- **Post-purchase rationalization** — people justify a choice after committing. **Use:** provide onboarding that helps users realize genuine value. **Risk:** do not exploit sunk costs to prevent cancellation.
- **Conjunction fallacy** — a detailed story feels more likely than a simpler one. **Use:** keep claims proportionate to evidence. **Risk:** avoid impressive but unsupported narratives.
- **Information bias** — more information is assumed to improve decisions. **Use:** expose decision-relevant detail progressively. **Risk:** do not bury the answer under data.
- **Ambiguity effect** — uncertain options are avoided. **Use:** clarify unknowns, assumptions, and next steps. **Risk:** do not hide ambiguity behind confident copy.
- **Reactance** — perceived restriction creates resistance. **Use:** explain reasons, offer choice, and preserve control. **Risk:** avoid coercive gates and blocked exits.
- **System justification** — existing systems are seen as more legitimate because they exist. **Use:** explain why a change improves the user's job. **Risk:** legacy behavior is not automatically good UX.
- **Backfire effect** — correction can strengthen an identity-linked belief. **Use:** correct respectfully with evidence and an alternative explanation. **Risk:** do not shame users.
- **Zero-risk bias** — eliminating one small risk feels better than reducing larger total risk. **Use:** show total risk profile and tradeoffs. **Risk:** never imply zero risk when it is only partial.
- **Overconfidence effect** — confidence exceeds accuracy. **Use:** add previews, checks, and reversible actions. **Risk:** do not reward certainty without evidence.
- **Hard-easy effect** — confidence can be too low on hard tasks and too high on easy ones. **Use:** calibrate guidance and feedback to task difficulty. **Risk:** do not assume expert confidence equals competence.
- **Dunning-Kruger effect** — low expertise can impair self-assessment. **Use:** provide progressive learning and concrete feedback. **Risk:** avoid labeling users or designing humiliating gates.
- **Illusion of control** — people overestimate control over uncertain outcomes. **Use:** show what the system controls and what it cannot. **Risk:** do not sell control that does not exist.
- **Illusory superiority** — people rate themselves above average. **Use:** use objective feedback and benchmarks. **Risk:** avoid flattery as a substitute for evidence.
- **Planning fallacy** — people underestimate time and effort. **Use:** show realistic estimates, dependencies, and progress. **Risk:** do not promise instant completion.
- **Pro-innovation bias** — new technology is overvalued and drawbacks underweighted. **Use:** show fit, limitations, and migration cost. **Risk:** novelty is not a user benefit by itself.

## Social behavior, identity, and motivation

- **Bizarreness effect** — unusual information is remembered better. **Use:** use distinctiveness for genuinely important concepts. **Risk:** novelty must not reduce clarity.
- **Humor effect** — humor can improve recall and warmth. **Use:** use it sparingly in low-risk contexts. **Risk:** never joke around errors, accessibility, payment, or loss.
- **Empathy gap** — people underestimate how decisions feel in another state. **Use:** design for stressed, rushed, or future users. **Risk:** test with realistic conditions.
- **Prejudice** — learned negative judgments affect interpretation. **Use:** audit language, imagery, and defaults for exclusion. **Risk:** never encode stereotypes as personas.
- **Implicit stereotypes** — unconscious associations influence behavior. **Use:** use inclusive examples and neutral labels. **Risk:** review imagery and ranking systems.
- **In-group favoritism** — people favor perceived group members. **Use:** make collaboration roles and norms explicit. **Risk:** do not create insider-only product access.
- **Stereotype** — simplified group beliefs distort judgment. **Use:** model individual needs and allow user control. **Risk:** do not use demographic shortcuts without evidence.
- **Fundamental attribution error** — behavior is attributed to character rather than context. **Use:** show system causes and environmental constraints. **Risk:** avoid blame-heavy errors and admin tools.
- **Group attribution error** — a group is judged by one member's behavior. **Use:** separate individual and aggregate signals. **Risk:** do not punish whole teams for one event.
- **Authority bias** — authority signals are trusted more than evidence. **Use:** show credentials with relevant sourcing. **Risk:** authority must not replace proof or consent.
- **Halo effect** — one positive trait colors other judgments. **Use:** separate brand polish from product evidence. **Risk:** do not let visual quality hide poor usability.
- **Bandwagon effect** — perceived popularity influences adoption. **Use:** show real, relevant adoption data. **Risk:** never fabricate numbers or social activity.
- **Placebo** — expectations can change perceived outcomes. **Use:** set helpful expectations and measure actual outcomes. **Risk:** do not claim efficacy without evidence.
- **Just-world fallacy** — outcomes are assumed to reflect deservingness. **Use:** explain system decisions and appeals. **Risk:** do not blame users for inaccessible outcomes.
- **In-group favoritism** — group identity increases preference for insiders. **Use:** support belonging without exclusion. **Risk:** avoid insider language that blocks newcomers.
- **Social desirability bias** — people report what looks acceptable. **Use:** make feedback private, easy, and nonjudgmental. **Risk:** do not treat self-report as ground truth.
- **Third-person effect** — people think persuasion affects others more than themselves. **Use:** include self-reflection in consent and awareness flows. **Risk:** do not assume users are immune to manipulation.
- **Consensus bias** — personal preferences are assumed to be common. **Use:** validate with diverse users and expose alternatives. **Risk:** do not design only for the team.
- **Barnum effect** — vague descriptions feel personally accurate. **Use:** make personas and recommendations concrete and editable. **Risk:** avoid generic personality claims.
- **Mental accounting** — people treat money or effort in separate mental buckets. **Use:** show total impact across plans and workflows. **Risk:** do not split costs to disguise the total.
- **Normality bias** — people underestimate unusual threats. **Use:** provide calm contingency guidance. **Risk:** do not wait for crisis signals to communicate risk.
- **Survival bias** — visible successes hide failures and exits. **Use:** include churn, failure, and non-completion data. **Risk:** never present only winners.
- **Appeal to novelty** — newness is mistaken for improvement. **Use:** state what materially improves and why. **Risk:** do not redesign for novelty alone.
- **Not invented here** — outside ideas are rejected because they are external. **Use:** evaluate proposals by evidence and fit. **Risk:** do not privilege internal work automatically.
- **Endowment effect** — ownership increases perceived value. **Use:** preview saved work and protect user-created data. **Risk:** do not make export or deletion difficult.
- **IKEA effect** — effort invested increases attachment. **Use:** let users contribute meaningful setup and see progress. **Risk:** never create needless configuration to lock users in.
- **Generation effect** — self-created information is remembered better. **Use:** let users create examples, goals, or labels when helpful. **Risk:** do not offload essential system work onto users.
- **Escalation of commitment** — prior investment drives continued commitment. **Use:** support honest review and easy pivots. **Risk:** never use sunk cost to trap users.
- **Loss aversion** — losses feel larger than equivalent gains. **Use:** make consequences and recovery explicit. **Risk:** avoid fear-based retention.
- **Hyperbolic discounting** — immediate rewards outweigh larger future rewards. **Use:** show near-term steps toward long-term value. **Risk:** do not exploit impulse with harmful defaults.
- **Positivity effect** — positive information can be remembered more with age. **Use:** keep guidance constructive and actionable. **Risk:** optimism must not hide risks.
- **Spotlight effect** — social exposure feels larger than it is. **Use:** provide privacy and preview controls. **Risk:** avoid public-by-default actions.
- **Illusory superiority** — users may overestimate competence. **Use:** provide objective checks and supportive learning. **Risk:** do not flatter users into risky actions.
- **Risk compensation** — people may take more risk when they feel safer. **Use:** explain safety limits and residual risk. **Risk:** do not imply protection is complete.

## Application rule

Each production UX review must reference an exact pattern-card heading from this
file. If a concept is not represented by a card, record `No pattern selected`
instead of inventing a source-backed claim. A selected card must be classified
as `source-backed`, `adapted`, or `hypothesis`, and its finding must include
priority and validation method in `ux-review-template.md`.

## Additional cards

- **Out-group homogeneity** — outsiders are seen as more alike than insiders. **Use:** allow individual roles, needs, and preferences to remain visible. **Risk:** do not collapse users into broad segments.
- **Functional fixedness** — familiar use of an object blocks alternative uses. **Use:** provide examples and alternate affordances when a control is unfamiliar. **Risk:** do not assume an icon has only one meaning.
- **Subadditivity effect** — a whole can be judged as less than the sum of its parts when grouped poorly. **Use:** show totals and relationships between sections. **Risk:** avoid fragmented metrics that obscure the total.
- **The Magical Number 7±2** — working memory is limited. **Use:** chunk and group choices instead of presenting a flat list. **Risk:** treat this as a heuristic, not a literal limit.
- **Self-reference effect** — personally relevant information is remembered better. **Use:** connect guidance to the user's goal, role, or data. **Risk:** personalization must be accurate and transparent.

For any UI change, select three to seven cards, state the user problem, propose the interface treatment, define the ethical boundary, and specify how to validate the result. If no card maps cleanly to the problem, do not force a cognitive explanation.
